# Cognitive Biases in Collaboration

## Heuristics and Anchoring

{/* Source tracking: Tversky & Kahneman (1974), abstract; Aranda & Easterbrook (2005), software estimation experiment. */}

Tversky and Kahneman (1974) described three judgment heuristics: representativeness, availability, and adjustment from an anchor. They called these heuristics highly economical and usually effective, but the source of systematic and predictable errors. A cognitive bias, in this sense, is such a predictable error rather than random noise.

Anchoring is the third heuristic. When a relevant number is available, people start from it and adjust. The adjustment is frequently insufficient (Aranda and Easterbrook, 2005), so the final judgment stays closer to the starting value than it should. In software work the starting value is often a figure someone says aloud: a deadline, a budget, a colleague's guess.

Aranda and Easterbrook (2005) tested this in software estimation. Participants estimated the same project after seeing different suggested figures, and a high anchor shifted the estimates upward compared with a control group. The sample was small, and the effect of the low anchor was not significant, so the finding supports "an anchor pulls estimates toward it", not "the first number decides the result". Field effects may also differ from laboratory ones.

## Confirmation Bias

{/* Source tracking: Nickerson (1998), abstract; Lord, Lepper & Preston (1984), abstract. */}

Nickerson (1998) reviewed confirmation bias across many settings. The term denotes seeking or interpreting evidence in ways that favour existing beliefs, expectations, or a hypothesis already held. An engineer who prefers a library reads its benchmarks charitably, searches for success stories, and treats complaints as edge cases.

Lord, Lepper and Preston (1984) compared two instructions. Asking people to consider the opposite corrected biased judgment more effectively than asking them to be fair and unbiased. This is one article reporting two laboratory experiments, and only its abstract was used here, so the result is encouraging but not a proven team practice. It suggests that a written list of reasons the favoured option might fail is a better prompt than a general call for objectivity.

## Biased Information Sharing in Groups

{/* Source tracking: Stasser & Titus (1985), abstract. */}

Groups add a second problem: what members know does not automatically reach the discussion. Stasser and Titus (1985) gave members partial, deliberately skewed candidate descriptions to read alone before a group discussion. Groups tended to side with the candidate a plurality had favoured at the start, not the strongest one. The authors explained this with a biased sampling model: the conversation gravitates to commonly held facts and to facts that fit members' prior preferences, so talking tends to entrench the distorted picture rather than repair it. The evidence here is one simulation of a political caucus, so the lesson should not be stretched to every meeting.

Social loafing, a drop in individual effort within a group, is sometimes listed beside these biases. It is an effort effect, not a judgment bias, and it falls outside the scope of this topic.

## Example: A Hypothetical Estimation Meeting

Week 3 of a hypothetical project. Tomás, the product owner, opens the estimation meeting by saying that the payment-retry feature "is about a two-week job". Amira, a developer, had privately reasoned the work was larger, but the first spoken figure now frames every discussion, and the team's numbers cluster near Tomás's. This is anchoring: adjustment from his number is insufficient.

The team lead, Dana, changes the process. For the next feature she asks each developer to write an estimate privately and reveal all of them at once, as in planning poker, and the lowest and highest estimators explain their reasoning. Amira's higher figure now appears beside the others, and the explanation exposes a missing migration step that Tomás had not considered. The estimate rises, and the schedule is set on the revised number.

## Evidence on Planning Poker

{/* Source tracking: Molokken-Ostvold & Haugen (2007), abstract. */}

Planning poker is a plausible mitigation, not a proven cure. Molokken-Ostvold and Haugen (2007) reported that tasks estimated with planning poker produced group estimates less optimistic and more accurate than a mechanical combination of individual estimates, while tasks estimated by individual experts reached similar accuracy. Their study does not isolate anchoring, so it does not show that simultaneous reveal removes it. The evidence is therefore weak to mixed. This suggests treating simultaneous reveal as one inexpensive safeguard and checking estimates against past actuals.

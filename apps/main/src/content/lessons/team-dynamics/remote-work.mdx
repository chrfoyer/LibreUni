# Remote Work: Beyond Video Calls

Remote work changes how a team coordinates effort across space and time. Moving a meeting from a room to a video call does not change those mechanics. The team still has to decide who knows what, when decisions are made, and how a colleague finds out that a dependency has slipped. Remote and hybrid teams fail when they copy the rituals of co-located work without replacing the informal signals that made those rituals unnecessary.

## What Co-location Provided Without Asking

In an office, much coordination happens without a deliberate act. An engineer sees a teammate stuck at a whiteboard, overhears a conversation about a failing build, or notices that a colleague has not left their desk all day. These signals are weak individually, but together they keep work visible.

Distributed teams lose most of these signals. Research on distributed teams shows that when members lack shared context, they conflict more often and resolve conflicts less well. Hinds and Mortensen (2005) found that spontaneous communication and shared context reduced the difficulty of conflict in geographically distributed teams, and that shared identity by itself did not compensate for their absence.

## Four Core Challenges

1. **The visibility gap.** Without physical presence, a manager or teammate cannot easily tell whether work is progressing. The usual response is more status meetings, which consume time and add interruptions.
2. **Context switching.** Chat tools and calendar invitations make every member reachable at all times. Each interruption breaks a chain of thought, and the cost of recovering focus is often larger than the interruption itself.
3. **Social isolation.** Relationships formed through casual contact are harder to create remotely. Weak relationships make it harder to ask for help or disagree.
4. **Time zone asymmetry.** When teammates are eight or more hours apart, a question asked in the morning may not get an answer until the next day. Handoffs become a source of delay and error.

## Practices That Address the Challenges

Remote work is not inherently less productive. A randomized experiment at a Chinese travel agency found that employees who worked from home were more productive than office-based colleagues (Bloom et al., 2015). The gains depend on how the work is organized, which is why the practices below matter. Most effective remote practices reduce dependence on real-time communication without removing human contact.

- **Asynchronous first.** Default to written communication in a shared, searchable place such as an issue tracker, a design document, or a team chat channel with clear threading. A written update lets a teammate in another time zone act without waiting for a meeting.
- **Intentional over-communication.** Narrate work in progress. A short note that says what is being attempted, what is blocked, and when the author expects to know more replaces the ambient signals of an office.
- **Protected focus time and office hours.** Set specific periods for synchronous collaboration and encourage long stretches without meetings. Office hours concentrate interruptions into predictable windows.
- **Written decisions.** Record decisions and their reasons in a durable place. A decision that exists only in a call is invisible to anyone who was not there.
- **Explicit boundaries.** Publish working hours, response expectations, and out-of-office status so that people do not feel obliged to answer messages during rest.

Tools support these practices but do not create them. A team with weak norms will struggle with any tool, and a team with strong norms can work well with a small set of simple tools.

## Example: A Cross-Time-Zone Handoff

A payment service team has engineers in Lisbon and in Toronto. The Lisbon engineer finishes a migration at the end of the day and leaves no written status. The Toronto engineer starts work the next morning, finds the migration half-applied, and spends three hours reconstructing its state from logs before proceeding.

A better handoff takes ten minutes. The Lisbon engineer writes a short note: what was applied, what remains, which checks passed, and what the next step is. The Toronto engineer continues within minutes. The technical work is identical in both cases. The difference is whether the team's communication norms made the state of the work visible without a synchronous conversation.

# Designing Your Team Operating System

"Ways of working" is the set of practices a team uses to plan, coordinate, and deliver work. It includes how work is split, how priorities are set, how progress is seen, and how decisions about process itself are made. Every team has ways of working, whether or not it has chosen them. The useful question is whether the practices were designed to fit the team's work or inherited without examination.

## Three Common Frameworks

Frameworks package a set of practices with a rationale. Each one optimizes for different properties of delivery.

- **Scrum** organizes work into fixed-length sprints with a defined goal, a backlog, a daily synchronization meeting, and a sprint review and retrospective. The Scrum Guide (Schwaber & Sutherland, 2020) defines three accountabilities and five events. Scrum emphasizes predictability and regular inspection of progress, which suits work with clear goals that can be divided into increments.
- **Kanban** visualizes work on a board and limits the amount of work in progress at each stage. David Anderson (2010) developed it as a way to evolve existing processes gradually. Its main property is flow: a limit on work in progress exposes bottlenecks, and throughput becomes measurable. Kanban does not prescribe time-boxes, so it suits teams with unpredictable or interrupt-driven work, such as operations.
- **Shape Up** is described by Ryan Singer (2019). It replaces continuous backlog grooming with six-week cycles. Before a cycle starts, a small group shapes a problem into a bounded solution, identifying the risks and leaving out details the builders should decide. The team then works within a fixed time. Shape Up de-risks work before it starts, and it avoids the daily status ritual that some teams find unhelpful.

None of these is correct in general. Scrum's sprint boundaries fit work that can be finished in two weeks. Kanban's flow measures fit a support queue. Shape Up's upfront shaping fits product work where scope is the main uncertainty. A team that adopts a framework without understanding its assumptions often keeps the ceremonies and loses the purpose behind them.

## Example: Choosing Between Sprints and Flow

A platform team maintains internal tooling and also responds to incidents from other teams. Its sprints are repeatedly disrupted, and two of every five planned items are carried over. The team is not failing at Scrum. Its work arrives unpredictably, so fixed-length commitments are the wrong unit of planning.

A better response is to keep the team's planning cadence but use a board with a work-in-progress limit for the unplanned queue, and reserve a fixed share of capacity for incidents. The team then measures how long items take from start to finish, which gives it evidence for future planning. The change came from examining the work, not from switching to the most popular framework.

## The Retrospective

Every framework above includes some form of reflection, and the retrospective is its most important form. A team that does not examine its own process cannot improve it. Derby and Larsen (2006) describe retrospectives as structured conversations that surface what happened, why, and what to change.

A simple structure is enough.

1. **What went well?** Name practices to keep.
2. **What did not go well?** Describe specific events, not general complaints.
3. **What will we change?** Choose one or two actions, assign an owner, and check the result in the next retrospective.

The third question is where most retrospectives fail. A list of ten improvements that nobody owns produces no change. Actionable items should be specific enough that a team member could tell, at the next meeting, whether they were done.

## Continuous Improvement

Taiichi Ohno's Toyota Production System and the idea of kaizen, popularized outside Japan by Masaaki Imai (1986), treat small, continuous improvement as the responsibility of everyone in the process, not only of managers. Applied to a team, this means that a meeting that does not help should be changed, a review process that is too slow should be automated where possible, and the team should treat its own process as something it designs and revises.

A useful rule follows: the team owns the process, and the process should not own the team. When a practice starts to cost more than it gives, the team should be willing to change it.

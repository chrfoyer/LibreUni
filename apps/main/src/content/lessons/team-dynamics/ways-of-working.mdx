# Designing Your Team Operating System

"Ways of working" is the set of practices a team uses to plan, coordinate, and deliver work. It includes how work is split, how priorities are set, how progress is seen, and how decisions about process itself are made. Every team has ways of working, whether or not it has chosen them. The useful question is whether the practices were designed to fit the team's work or inherited without examination.

## Three Common Frameworks

{/* Source tracking: Schwaber & Sutherland (2020), Sprints and events; Singer (2019), chapters 1, 8, 13; Wikipedia contributors, Kanban (development) and Little's law (CC BY-SA 4.0); Little (1961). */}

Frameworks package a set of practices with a rationale. Each one optimizes for different properties of delivery.

- **Scrum** organizes work into Sprints, a backlog, and regular inspection. The Scrum Guide (Schwaber & Sutherland, 2020) defines three accountabilities (Developers, Product Owner, Scrum Master) and five events, and describes Sprints as fixed-length events of one month or less. Two weeks is a choice, not a rule. Scrum uses an iterative, incremental approach to optimize predictability (Schwaber & Sutherland, 2020), which suits work with clear goals that can be divided into increments.
- **Kanban** has two layers. The kanban mechanism comes from the Toyota Production System (Ohno, 1988). Anderson (2010) adapted it into the Kanban Method for knowledge work, whose two primary practices are to visualize work and to limit work in progress (Wikipedia contributors, 2026). The approach aims to improve the handling of system-level bottlenecks (Wikipedia contributors, 2026). Practitioners treat it as a fit for teams with interrupt-driven work, such as operations.
- **Shape Up** is described by Singer (2019). Work runs in six-week cycles, each followed by a two-week cool-down with no scheduled work, during which stakeholders decide which shaped pitches to bet on next. A small senior group works alongside the cycle teams, defining a solution's key elements before a project is considered ready for a bet (Singer, 2019). Singer reports that there are no daily meetings; progress is instead shown on hill charts, which separate the uphill phase of figuring out an approach from the downhill phase of execution. Singer (2019) reports that Basecamp found a two-week cycle too short for meaningful work, which is one team's view, not a general result.

The fit of each framework is a design heuristic from practitioners, not a tested finding, and no empirical comparison of the three is cited here. The heuristics are these: Scrum fits work with clear goals, Kanban's flow measures fit a support queue, and Shape Up's upfront shaping fits product work where scope is the main uncertainty.

Work in progress links these frameworks through Little's law (Little, 1961). In a stable system, average work in progress equals average throughput multiplied by average time in system (Wikipedia contributors, 2026). At constant throughput, more work in progress therefore means a longer average cycle time. This suggests that a team whose items take ever longer to finish should look at how much it has started before it looks at how fast it works.

## Continuous Improvement

{/* Source tracking: Wikipedia contributors, Kaizen (CC BY-SA 4.0), definition; Imai (1986), bibliographic record. */}

Wikipedia contributors (2026) define kaizen as "a concept in business studies which asserts that significant positive results may be achieved due to the cumulative effect of many, often small (and even trivial) improvements to all aspects of a company's operations" (CC BY-SA 4.0). Imai (1986) is a book on kaizen. In a team, the idea is that the process is itself an object of work: a meeting that no longer helps is changed, a slow review step is simplified, and the team treats its own process as something it designs and revises.

The retrospective is the routine mechanism by which a team revises its process.

## The Retrospective

{/* Source tracking: Derby & Larsen (2012), five phases, as described by Nettleton (2018); Schwaber & Sutherland (2020). */}

Derby and Larsen (2012), as summarized by Nettleton (2018), describe a retrospective in five phases: Set the Stage, Gather Data, Generate Insights, Decide What to Do, and Close. The three-question format (what went well, what did not, what will we change) is not their five phases.

In the Decide What to Do phase the group selects one or two action items (Nettleton, 2018). This suggests that an action item should be specific enough that a team member could tell, at the next retrospective, whether it was done.

## Example: Choosing Flow and Reviewing It

This is a hypothetical case. A platform team has a lead and four engineers. It maintains internal tooling and also responds to incidents from other teams. In week 3, its Sprints are repeatedly disrupted, and two of the five planned items are carried over.

In the retrospective, the lead sets the stage by asking everyone to describe the Sprint in one word. The team gathers data: two items carried over, both displaced by incidents. It generates an insight: the work arrives unpredictably, so fixed-length commitments are the wrong unit of planning. For the decision phase, it chooses one action. An engineer named Sam owns a trial of a board with a work-in-progress limit for the unplanned queue, and the team will reserve a fixed share of capacity for incidents. The team closes by agreeing to review the cycle time of finished items in week 7.

By week 7, the unplanned queue shows shorter start-to-finish times and fewer items are carried over, consistent with Little's law at constant throughput: less work is started at once. The change came from examining the work and assigning an owner.

## Exercise

Pick one practice your team uses, such as a standup, a review rule, or a planning meeting. Write down its purpose, the outcome the team currently gets from it, and one change that would improve the outcome, with a named owner. A good answer states how you would know, in four weeks, whether the change worked.

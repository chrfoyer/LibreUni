# How Teams Decide

A team decision has two parts: who holds the authority to decide, and how the group surfaces information and objections first. Both can fail.

## Decision Regimes

{/* Source tracking: Vroom & Yetton (1973) and Vroom & Jago (1988), overview of the five styles and the question set, read through a secondary summary. */}

Vroom and Yetton (1973) proposed a normative model, later developed with Jago (Vroom & Jago, 1988), that matches the way a leader involves a team to the situation. It names five styles, ranging from autocratic (AI, AII) through consultative (CI, CII) to group-based (GII). The leader chooses among them by answering yes/no questions about the decision, covering decision quality, the information available, and how much the team's commitment matters to carrying it out. The model is prescriptive, and this lesson cites no validation of its prescriptions.

Sociocracy 3.0 describes a different regime, consent. A proposal is accepted when it is "good enough for now and safe enough to try until the next review" (Bockelbrink, Priest & David, 2026). The pattern separates two kinds of reaction. An objection is a reasoned argument that reveals consequences or risks worth avoiding, or a worthwhile improvement, and it stops a proposal from becoming policy. A concern is an assumption that cannot yet be backed by reasoning or evidence, and it does not stop the proposal.

## Groupthink

{/* Source tracking: Janis (1982), concept and symptoms, read through a secondary summary; Esser (1998), abstract. */}

Groupthink is Janis's term for a mode of thinking in cohesive groups in which the drive for agreement overrides realistic appraisal of alternatives (Janis, 1982). Janis's listed symptoms include direct pressure on members who question the group and self-appointed mindguards, who shield the group from dissenting information.

The evidence for the model is incomplete. In a review of the research, Esser (1998) concluded that groupthink research has had considerable heuristic value, that the empirical literature is small but growing, and that most of the theoretical ideas it stimulated have yet to be tested. This suggests treating groupthink as a useful lens for diagnosing a meeting, not as a settled law of group behaviour.

## Counteracting Groupthink

{/* Source tracking: Janis (1982), prevention measures, read through a secondary summary; Nemeth, Brown & Rogers (2001), abstract; Diehl & Stroebe (1987), abstract. */}

Janis (1982) proposed several safeguards. The leader withholds a personal opinion when assigning the task, which is leader impartiality. Each member acts as a critical evaluator. A devil's advocate is assigned, a different person each meeting. Independent groups can tackle the same problem, and outside experts can be consulted.

The assigned devil's advocate has a weakness. Nemeth, Brown and Rogers (2001) compared three forms of devil's advocate, differing in whether the advocate's position was known and consistent, known and inconsistent, or unknown, with an authentic minority, and found the authentic minority superior to all three. They also report that an earlier study found the devil's advocate fostered thinking aimed at bolstering the initial viewpoint. The evidence is one line of experiments, so it is suggestive: it implies that making room for people who actually disagree beats a rotating role.

Diehl and Stroebe (1987) ran four experiments and found that production blocking, in which only one person can speak at a time, explained most of the lower output of real brainstorming groups. That finding covers idea generation, not decision meetings, so applying it to decisions, for example by collecting written ideas first, is an inference.

## Role Frameworks: DACI and RAPID

{/* Source tracking: Atlassian (n.d.), DACI play; Rogers & Blenko (2006), Bain page. */}

Role frameworks answer the first question, who decides. Atlassian (n.d.) publishes DACI as a Team Playbook play with four roles. The Driver collects stakeholders and information, sets the scope, and gets a decision made by the agreed date. The Approver is one person, and only that person makes the decision. Contributors have subject knowledge and a voice but not a vote. The Informed are told the result.

RAPID, from Rogers and Blenko (2006), names five roles: Recommend, Agree, Perform, Input, and Decide. The authors note that the roles are not carried out in that order. Agree differs from anything in DACI, because those who agree must accept a recommendation before it moves forward, and a person who vetoes must offer an alternative or escalate to the person with the Decide role. Both come from practitioners, and this lesson cites no controlled evaluation of either.

## Example: The Challenger Launch Decision

{/* Source tracking: Rogers Commission (1986), Vol. 1, Ch. V; Esser & Lindoerfer (1989), abstract; Vaughan (1996), chapter contents. */}

The primary source for this case is the report of the Rogers Commission. Published in 1986, it concluded that the contractor Thiokol's management changed course and backed launching flight 51-L at the urging of NASA's Marshall center, against what its engineers thought. In testimony, a manager asked an engineer for a change of role: "take off his engineering hat and put on his management hat" (Rogers Commission, 1986). One engineer testified that no engineer spoke in favour of launching. The Commission found "a serious flaw in the decision making process" (Rogers Commission, 1986).

Whether to call that flaw groupthink is contested. Esser and Lindoerfer (1989) coded statements from the Commission's report as instances of groupthink antecedents and consequences. Positive instances were twice as frequent as negative ones, and the ratio rose in the 24 hours before launch, which they describe as consistent with groupthink. Because the coding rests on the same report, it is not independent evidence. Vaughan (1996) gives a different account, normalization of deviance, in which departures from proper practice become culturally accepted. She treats Challenger as her original example.

On the groupthink reading, the dissent was pressured away and the recommendation flipped from no-launch to launch. This suggests that a structure protecting objections, such as a Contributor or Input role that the Approver must hear, would at least have kept the engineers' view on the record.

## Exercise

Choose a go/no-go decision, such as releasing a risky change on a Friday. Write a one-page decision record that does four things: names the Vroom and Yetton style you would use and justifies it with two of their question types; assigns DACI roles to named people and maps each to a RAPID role, marking where RAPID's Agree or Perform has no DACI counterpart; states one Janis safeguard and how it would be run; and says how an objection would be recorded and answered. A good answer has exactly one Approver, keeps Contributors separate from the Approver, and defines objections as reasoned arguments.

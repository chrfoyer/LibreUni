# Why We Work: Motivation Theory

A manager who wants more output from an engineering team has to decide what to change: pay, the work itself, the way performance is measured, or the degree of freedom people have. Motivation theories answer this by separating what people want from work into different kinds, and a meta-analysis of rewards (Cerasoli, Nicklin & Ford, 2014) found that the role of intrinsic motivation in performance depends on how incentives are tied to performance. Three bodies of work are compared here: Herzberg's two-factor theory, self-determination theory, and meta-analyses of rewards.

## Two-Factor Theory

{/* Source tracking: Herzberg, Mausner & Snyderman (1959), two-factor theory as summarized in secondary sources; House & Wigdor (1967), title-level only. */}

Herzberg, Mausner and Snyderman (1959) proposed that one set of job characteristics leads to satisfaction while a separate set leads to dissatisfaction, so the two are not opposite ends of one scale. In the usual summary, **hygiene factors** include company policy and administration, supervision, interpersonal relations, working conditions, and salary. When they are absent they cause dissatisfaction, but their presence does not produce satisfaction. **Motivators** include recognition, achievement, responsibility, advancement, growth opportunities, and the work itself.

Salary is therefore classed as a hygiene factor. A common inference is that a raise will not make miserable work satisfying for long. That inference is not a finding of the theory's tests.

The evidence is contested. House and Wigdor (1967) published a review of the evidence for the theory and a criticism of it.  The theory is influential, but it is better treated as a prompt for questions about a team's work than as a settled law.

## Self-Determination Theory

{/* Source tracking: Ryan & Deci (2000), abstract and sections on needs and autonomy. */}

Ryan and Deci (2000) describe self-determination theory as proposing three basic psychological needs, for autonomy, competence, and relatedness. Satisfying them supports intrinsic motivation and well-being, and thwarting them is linked to diminished motivation and well-being.

- **Autonomy** is the sense of acting by one's own volition, whether the act is dependent on others or independent. Ryan and Deci (2000) state that it is not independence: a person can act with volition while depending on others.
- **Competence** concerns feeling competent. The authors report that optimal challenges and effectance-promoting feedback facilitate intrinsic motivation.
- **Relatedness** is the need to feel belongingness and connectedness with others (Ryan & Deci, 2000).

Ryan and Deci (2000) also report that Baard, Deci and Ryan (1998) found workers' need satisfaction predicted performance and well-being at work. This comes from a review by the theory's own authors, so it shows a broad research programme, not a universal law.

## Rewards and Intrinsic Motivation

{/* Source tracking: Deci, Koestner & Ryan (1999), abstract; Cerasoli, Nicklin & Ford (2014), abstract; Kerr (1975), abstract; Ryan & Deci (2000), full text on intrinsic interest. */}

Intrinsic motivation, in this theory, applies to activities that hold interest through novelty, challenge, or aesthetic value (Ryan & Deci, 2000). Deci, Koestner and Ryan (1999) meta-analysed 128 experiments on extrinsic rewards. Rewards contingent on engagement, completion, or performance each lowered intrinsic motivation as measured by free choice (effect sizes of d = -0.40 for engagement, -0.36 for completion, and -0.28 for performance), and so did all tangible and all expected rewards. Positive feedback enhanced it (d = 0.33). This is the **undermining effect**, and its outcome is intrinsic motivation, not job performance.

Cerasoli, Nicklin and Ford (2014) pooled 183 samples (N = 212,468) from school, work, and physical domains to ask how intrinsic motivation and incentives relate to performance itself. Quality of performance was better explained by intrinsic motivation, while quantity was better explained by incentives. How the incentive was attached also mattered: when pay was directly tied to performance, intrinsic motivation counted for less (crowding out), and when it was tied indirectly, it counted for more. Incentives and intrinsic motivation, the authors write, are "not necessarily antagonistic". Neither meta-analysis tested engineering work specifically.

Kerr (1975) described organizations whose rewards go to one behavior while the rewarder hopes for a different one. This suggests that a measure chosen for convenience, such as tickets closed, may shape behavior more than the quality the team actually wants.

## Pink's Drive

{/* Source tracking: Pink (2009), Open Library table of contents and secondary summary; Cerasoli, Nicklin & Ford (2014), abstract. */}

Pink (2009) organises motivation as autonomy, mastery, and purpose. The book is a popularization, not a peer-reviewed study. Its three elements are not the three needs of self-determination theory: purpose is not one of SDT's needs, and the shared word autonomy should not hide that the framings differ. Pink's claim that bonuses help only with basic mechanical tasks comes from a popular book. Cerasoli et al. (2014) did not compare simple and complex tasks, but found incentives were the better predictor of quantity of performance.

## Example: A Bonus per Ticket

This scenario is hypothetical. In week 1, a team lead named Dana announces a bonus for each support ticket an engineer closes. By week 3, closed tickets have risen, but Priya, a senior engineer who used to spend time on thorough fixes, now closes the quickest tickets first. By week 6, reviewers such as Marcus report more reopened tickets and shallower fixes.

The scheme paid off quantity while the team hoped for quality, the pattern Kerr (1975) described, and the bonus was tied directly to performance, the condition under which Cerasoli et al. (2014) found intrinsic motivation mattered less. Dana drops the per-ticket bonus and instead asks the engineers to choose which recurring ticket causes to remove. This suggests the lead restored volition and a challenge to the work. By week 10, reopened tickets fall, though the team now closes fewer tickets in total.

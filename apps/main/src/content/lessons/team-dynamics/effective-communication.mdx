# Effective Communication

Communication in engineering transfers mental models. When one engineer describes a design, the goal is that the listener builds a model of the system that matches the speaker's. Mismatches between those models surface later, usually in code or production.

## Grounding and Media Constraints

{/* Source tracking: Clark & Brennan (1991), grounding and the eight media constraints. */}

Clark and Brennan (1991) describe communication as a process of *grounding*: the collective process by which participants try to reach mutual belief that the partners understood what was meant, well enough for the current purposes. They also propose a principle of least collaborative effort: participants try to minimize the work both do until a contribution is mutually accepted.

The medium decides what grounding costs, because it determines which techniques are available. Clark and Brennan (1991) list eight constraints a medium may impose: copresence (sharing a physical environment), visibility (seeing each other), audibility (communicating by speaking), cotemporality (the receiver gets a message at roughly the time the sender produces it; letters and email lack it), simultaneity (sending and receiving at once), sequentiality (turns cannot get out of sequence), reviewability (the receiver can reread messages; speech fades quickly), and revisability (the sender can edit privately before sending, as email allows).

Grounding techniques include acknowledgments such as head nods, try markers that present a provisional utterance and ask for confirmation, and installments that cut a difficult utterance into pieces checked one at a time. In keyboard teleconferencing, timing an acknowledgment precisely is difficult, so its cost is higher.

These constraints are a taxonomy from psycholinguistics, not a tested quantitative result, so they work as a model for reasoning about channels. A pull request comment, by the same reasoning, lacks cotemporality but gains reviewability and revisability. The author must anticipate misreadings.

## Choosing a Channel

{/* Source tracking: Dennis, Fuller & Valacich (2008), media synchronicity theory, abstract. */}

Dennis, Fuller, and Valacich (2008) propose media synchronicity theory. They define synchronicity as "a shared pattern of coordinated behavior among individuals as they work together" and name five media capabilities, among them symbol sets and rehearsability, that influence its development. Their propositions refer to media supporting lower or higher synchronicity, which treats synchronicity as a matter of degree. This lesson's own inference is that a chat thread can behave almost like a conversation or almost like a mailbox, depending on use.

The theory separates two communication processes, conveyance and convergence. It proposes that lower-synchronicity media should support conveyance better and higher-synchronicity media should support convergence better, and that performance improves when people use a variety of media rather than one. Dennis et al. (2008) state these as propositions ("should result in"), so the theory is a model awaiting testing in each setting, not an established effect. This suggests a working rule: announce a non-urgent change in writing, and settle a contested design in a live conversation followed by a written record.

## Giving Feedback in Code Review

{/* Source tracking: Sadowski et al. (2018), Section 4 themes; Egelman et al. (2020), abstract. */}

Sadowski et al. (2018) studied code review at Google through 12 interviews, a survey of 44 respondents, and review logs for 9 million reviewed changes. Interviewees expected four things from review: education, maintaining norms, gatekeeping, and accident prevention, plus tracking history. Finding defects matters, but it is not the only aim. The data come from one company, so they describe a practice and do not establish a general law.

Review is also an interpersonal exchange. Egelman et al. (2020) surveyed 1,317 developers at one company and cross-referenced the answers with review logs. They call negative interpersonal experiences in review *pushback* and report that such interactions may ultimately lead developers to abandon projects. Their finding is that pushback is relatively rare but has negative repercussions when it occurs. The evidence is correlational and limited to one organization. It suggests that a review comment deserves the same care as the code it addresses.

Four habits follow from the grounding model, framed here as inferences, not findings:

- **Describe the code, not the person.** "This function mixes parsing and validation" is more useful than "You made this hard to follow."
- **Explain the reason.** "Use a map here, because lookups happen in a hot loop" gives the author something to evaluate.
- **Mark blocking issues.** Authors who cannot tell required changes from suggestions must guess.
- **Ask before asserting.** "Is there a reason this handles only the first element?" may reveal context the reviewer lacked.

## Active Listening

{/* Source tracking: Wikipedia (2026), Active listening (CC BY-SA 4.0), techniques section. */}

Wikipedia (2026) names three basic techniques: paraphrasing, reflecting emotion, and open-ended questions. Paraphrasing puts the speaker's message into the listener's own words to show understanding and continue the discussion. This lesson makes no claim about how well the techniques work. In engineering, a paraphrase such as "So the concern is that the cache can return stale data during deployment?" is a grounding move: it lets the speaker correct a misunderstanding before it becomes a design decision.

## Example: A Terse Review Comment (Hypothetical)

In week 2, Priya opens a pull request and her reviewer Tomas comments, "Rewrite this." Priya replies twice in the thread, each time guessing at his objection, and neither message is grounded, because Tomas's comment gives no reason. In week 3 Tomas proposes a ten-minute call, a higher-synchronicity medium suited to convergence. He paraphrases, "You are worried that the retry loop hides failures?" and Priya corrects him: her worry is latency. Tomas then rewrites the comment in the thread: "Blocking: this retry loop swallows errors. Suggestion, not blocking: use exponential backoff." The comment now carries a reason and marks what blocks merging. The call converged the models, and the written comment conveyed the result to the rest of the team.

## Exercise

Pick one channel your team uses, such as pull request comments, a chat channel, or a stand-up. Write a short table that maps it onto the eight constraints, marking each as present, absent, or partial. Then name one grounding technique (acknowledgments, try markers, or installments) that becomes costly in that channel and one practice that offsets the cost. A good answer covers all eight constraints, justifies each partial mark with a concrete behavior, and links the costly technique to a specific missing constraint.

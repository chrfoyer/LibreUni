# The Foundation of Learning Teams

Engineering work depends on information that is uncomfortable to share: an unrealistic schedule, a design flaw noticed late, a metric that looks wrong. Whether people share it depends on what they expect to happen to them afterwards. Psychological safety names that expectation.

## Definition and Origins

{/* Source tracking: Edmondson (1999), abstract; Edmondson and Lei (2014), abstract; Edmondson (2018), chapter 1 excerpt. */}

Edmondson (1999) introduced the construct of team psychological safety, in the abstract's words, "a shared belief held by members of a team that the team is safe for interpersonal risk taking". In 51 work teams at one manufacturing company, Edmondson (1999) found that team psychological safety was associated with learning behavior, and that learning behavior mediated the link between psychological safety and team performance.

The term itself is older. Edmondson (2018) reports that Schein and Bennis addressed psychological safety in a 1965 book, in connection with the uncertainty of organizational change, and that Kahn studied it alongside meaningfulness as a condition for engagement at work in 1990. Edmondson and Lei (2014) describe a first exploration in the 1960s and a renaissance from the 1990s. The 1999 contribution is therefore the team-level construct, not the words.

The construct describes a team, not a person. A confident engineer can work in a team where speaking up is dangerous, and a quiet one in a team where it is normal.

## Why Better Teams Can Report More Errors

{/* Source tracking: Edmondson (1996), abstract; Edmondson (2018), chapter 1 excerpt. */}

Edmondson (1996) studied patient care groups in two hospitals and found that the groups differed systematically, both in how often errors occurred and in how likely members were to detect and learn from them. In her later account, Edmondson (2018) describes a puzzle: units she rated as stronger teams appeared to make more mistakes, not fewer. She interpreted this as a reporting difference, writing that good teams "don't make more mistakes; they report more", and she says a research assistant who did not know the error counts found differences in how comfortable units were discussing mistakes that were almost perfectly correlated with the detected rates.

This is Edmondson's interpretation, and the 1996 abstract alone does not state it. This suggests a rule for reading incident data: a low count can mean few errors or little disclosure, and the count alone does not say which.

## Evidence from Project Aristotle

{/* Source tracking: Google re:Work (2015), Understand team effectiveness guide; Duhigg (2016); Frazier et al. (2017), abstract. */}

Google's Project Aristotle studied 180 teams, 115 engineering project teams and 65 sales pods. Google re:Work (2015) lists five dynamics in order of importance: psychological safety, dependability, structure and clarity, meaning, and impact. Duhigg (2016) gives the conclusion as "Google's data indicated that psychological safety, more than anything else, was critical to making a team work."

The evidence has limits. The re:Work guide describes over 35 statistical models on hundreds of variables but, as far as this lesson's sources show, reports no effect sizes, and no peer-reviewed account was found. This is single-company, summary-level evidence. A broader evidence base is the meta-analysis by Frazier et al. (2017), which aggregates "136 independent samples representing over 22,000 individuals and nearly 5,000 groups"; its abstract states the question of whether psychological safety influences task performance and citizenship behavior beyond leader relations and engagement, and this lesson reports no results from it.

## Leader Behaviors

{/* Source tracking: Edmondson (2018), table of contents; Lunney and Lueder (2016), postmortem culture chapter. */}

The table of contents of Edmondson (2018) groups a leader's toolkit under three headings: set the stage, invite participation, and respond productively. One way to read these headings, as this lesson's own reading and not Edmondson's, is by timing: before work starts, when asking for input, and in the moment after someone speaks.

Blameless postmortems apply the same idea to incidents. Lunney and Lueder (2016) state that a blameless postmortem is aimed at "identifying the contributing causes of the incident without indicting any individual or team for bad or inappropriate behavior". They describe blameless postmortems as a tenet of SRE culture.

## Example: Two Reviews of the Same Outage

This is a hypothetical case. In week 1, a deployment causes a four-hour outage on a payments service. Dana, the team lead, opens the first postmortem by asking which engineer approved the change. Marcus, the reviewer, had written a concern in a code comment but says nothing. By week 5, near-misses have stopped appearing in postmortems, and in week 6 Marcus notices a similar risk and stays silent.

In week 7, Dana changes the format to a blameless postmortem. The group examines the review process, which let a change merge without a rollback plan, and finds that Marcus's comment was never seen by the approver. The fix is a review checklist, and Marcus is thanked for raising the concern. In week 9, a different engineer reports a near-miss within the hour. The change in team psychological safety shows in the outcome: the information that was withheld in weeks 1 to 6 now reaches the team while it is still cheap to act on.

## Exercise

Recall a team in which a problem stayed hidden. Write one paragraph naming the specific response, by a leader or colleague, that discouraged disclosure. Then describe a replacement response, stating when it happens (before work, when asking, or after someone speaks, following this lesson's timing reading) and which of the three toolkit headings it matches. A good answer keeps the standard for the work unchanged and shows what the speaker would observe.

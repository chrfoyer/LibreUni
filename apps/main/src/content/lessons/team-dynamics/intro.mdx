# The Human Element of Engineering

A design can be sound while the team that must build it withholds bad news, avoids a difficult decision, or waits for someone else to act. Team dynamics is the study of how people in a group interact, coordinate, influence one another, and produce outcomes that no single member could produce alone.

The field draws on organizational psychology, sociology, and management research. Its working assumption is that group behavior follows patterns, and that deliberate choices about structure, communication, and leadership can change them.

## Why Team Dynamics Matters for Engineers

{/* Source tracking: Kozlowski & Ilgen (2006), abstract; Woolley et al. (2010), editor summary; Credé & Howardson (2017), abstract. */}

Organizations have restructured work around teams, which has made the effectiveness of teams an important concern for organizations (Kozlowski & Ilgen, 2006). Woolley et al. (2010) reported a statistical factor they called collective intelligence, which reflects how well a group performs across a diverse set of problem-solving tasks. They found that its main contributors were the general ability of the members and a propensity toward social sensitivity.

This evidence is contested. Credé and Howardson (2017) reexamined data from six published samples and concluded that "empirical support for the construct is generally weak" (Credé & Howardson, 2017), because the general factor explains little variance in performance on many group tasks. The factor is best read as a hypothesis about group performance, not a settled measurement.

## Two Costs of Working in Groups

{/* Source tracking: Brooks (1995), via secondary sources; Latané, Williams, & Harkins (1979), abstract and secondary summary. */}

**Coordination cost.** Brooks (1995) argued that staffing up a software project that is already behind schedule delays it further. He gave three mechanisms. New members need time to become productive, and training them diverts existing staff. The number of communication paths grows roughly with the square of team size. Many tasks cannot be divided among more people, so extra hands do not shorten them. Brooks described the law as a rule of thumb drawn from experience, not as an empirical finding, so it is a warning about a mechanism and not a prediction of exact delay.

**Social loafing.** Latané, Williams, and Harkins (1979) ran experiments in which people clapped and shouted alone and in groups. Individual effort fell sizably in groups, and the authors called this decrease social loafing. It is in addition to losses from faulty coordination of group efforts, so the two are separate effects. Participants who shouted alone but believed others were shouting with them also reduced their effort. When individual inputs are not identifiable, a person may work less hard. These were physical tasks in a laboratory, so applying the result to engineering work is an extension. It suggests that making individual contributions visible, for example through reviewable commits, may reduce the effect.

## Blameless Postmortems

{/* Source tracking: Lunney & Lueder (2016), chapter text. */}

After an incident, a team can look for a culprit or look for causes. Lunney and Lueder (2016) describe the blameless postmortem, which is aimed at "identifying the contributing causes of the incident without indicting any individual or team for bad or inappropriate behavior".

## Example: One Incident, Two Explanations

Consider a hypothetical production outage in week 3 of a release cycle, caused by a configuration change. The author, Dana, pushed the change on Friday. The reviewer, Sam, approved it quickly because he assumed Dana had tested it in staging. Dana had skipped staging because the team's delivery metrics rewarded speed.

A technical explanation says the change was not tested. A dynamics explanation asks who assumed what, whether Dana could ask for a slower review, and whether the incentives favored verification. In a blameless postmortem, the team traces the outage to the unexamined review assumption and the speed incentive, and it adds a review checklist and a staging gate. Blaming Dana alone would have left both causes in place, and the same pattern would likely recur under a different technical guise.

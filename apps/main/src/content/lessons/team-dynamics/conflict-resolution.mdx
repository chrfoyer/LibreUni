# Navigating Friction

Conflict is a normal part of any team that does real work. Two engineers who disagree about a data model are doing their jobs. The aim is not to remove conflict but to keep it productive, so that disagreement improves the decision instead of damaging the team that makes it.

Research supports this distinction. Karen Jehn (1995) studied work groups and separated conflict into types with different effects. Task conflict, a disagreement about the content of the work, was associated with better decision quality when it was kept within bounds. Relationship conflict, a disagreement about personal matters, was associated with lower satisfaction and performance. The practical lesson is that the type of conflict matters more than its amount.

## Three Types of Conflict

1. **Task conflict** is disagreement over *what* to build or *how* to build it, such as an architectural choice or the scope of a release. It is often healthy when people can separate the idea from the person who proposed it.
2. **Process conflict** is disagreement over the team's ways of working, such as how estimates are made or who approves a merge. It is common during changes in practice and usually resolves through explicit agreement about the process.
3. **Relationship conflict** is personal friction, a lack of trust, or a history of unresolved slights. It is usually destructive because it makes every technical disagreement feel personal.

The three types often blur together. A technical disagreement becomes a relationship conflict when one party believes the other is acting in bad faith. Good resolution starts by identifying which type is occurring.

## Principled Negotiation

Roger Fisher and William Ury (1981) proposed an approach to negotiation that separates the people from the problem and focuses on interests rather than positions. A position is what someone says they want. An interest is why they want it. Two engineers may both insist on a particular framework, but one wants fast onboarding and the other wants long-term maintainability. Once the interests are visible, the team can search for options that satisfy both.

Applied to team conflict, the approach has four steps.

- **Separate the people from the problem.** Discuss the design, not the author. "This approach makes failure recovery harder" is about the system.
- **Focus on interests.** Ask what each person needs the solution to achieve before debating the solution.
- **Listen before proposing.** Make sure each party can state the other's view accurately before negotiating. Many stalemates persist because one side has not been heard.
- **Generate options together.** Look for solutions that serve both interests, and test them against objective criteria, such as latency targets or maintenance cost, rather than against who has more authority.

## Escalation Paths

Some disagreements cannot be resolved by discussion. Teams need a predictable path for those cases, so that a stalemate does not last for weeks.

1. **Peer review.** Ask a third engineer with relevant knowledge to assess the options and explain the tradeoffs.
2. **Experiment.** If the options can be tested cheaply, build small prototypes or run a benchmark and let the measurements decide.
3. **Decision owner.** Escalate to the person with authority over the decision, who makes the call and commits the team to it.

The third step needs a commitment from everyone: once the decision owner decides, the team proceeds with it, and disagreement is recorded but not relitigated in every meeting. Without that commitment, escalation only moves the conflict upward.

## Example: A Stalled Migration

Two senior engineers disagree about whether to migrate a service to a new message broker now or after a planned refactoring. In meetings, each argues the technical merits and neither moves. The team lead asks each engineer to state the other's concern. One says the other fears that a half-finished migration will leave two systems running for months. The other says that a refactoring first will delay the migration by a quarter. Both concerns are legitimate, and they point to a shared interest in not carrying two systems for long.

The team agrees on a time-boxed prototype of the migration on one non-critical path. The prototype reveals a serialization issue that makes the refactoring a prerequisite for part of the migration. The disagreement ends because the evidence settled the task question, and the interests were served by a plan that addressed both concerns.

# Navigating Friction

Conflict is a normal part of any team that does real work. Two engineers who disagree about a data model are doing their jobs. The research question is whether such disagreement helps or harms, and the answer is less comfortable than the popular view that a little friction is always productive.

## Types of Conflict and Their Effects

{/* Source tracking: Jehn (1995), abstract; Jehn (1997), abstract; De Dreu and Weingart (2003), examples and meta-analytic results; de Wit, Greer, and Jehn (2012), abstract; Wikipedia (2026), Conflict (process), definition. */}

Jehn (1995) studied 105 work groups and management teams and distinguished two types of conflict: task conflict and relationship conflict. De Dreu and Weingart (2003) give as examples of task conflict "conflicts about the distribution of resources, procedures and policies, and judgments and interpretation of facts", and of relationship conflict "conflicts about personal taste, political preferences, values, and interpersonal style". Process conflict is "disagreement over the group's approach to the task, its methods, and its group process" (Wikipedia, 2026). It overlaps with the "procedures and policies" in De Dreu and Weingart's task examples, which later work separates out. Jehn (1997), a qualitative study of six organizational work teams, added process conflict and found it detrimental to performance. Jehn (1997) also found that relationship conflict was detrimental to performance and satisfaction, and that groups with norms accepting task conflict but not relationship conflict were most effective.

Two meta-analyses summarize the quantitative evidence.

- De Dreu and Weingart (2003) found strong negative correlations of both relationship conflict and task conflict with both team performance and how satisfied members were. They found no evidence that the two types differ in their correlation with performance. This contradicted the prediction, common in both research and textbooks, that task conflict would help.
- de Wit et al. (2012) pooled 116 studies (8,880 groups). Contrasting their result with De Dreu and Weingart, they report "we did not find a strong and negative association between task conflict and group performance". Relationship and process conflict, by comparison, had stable negative relationships with group outcomes. Task conflict related more positively to performance in top management teams, with financial or decision-quality measures, and where task and relationship conflict were weakly associated.

The claim that task conflict is beneficial is therefore contested. Jehn (1995) found that disagreements about the task hurt groups doing very routine work, while in nonroutine work they did not hurt and sometimes helped. For individuals, she reports that "Relationship and task conflicts were negatively associated with individuals' satisfaction, liking of other group members, and intent to remain in the group". The safest reading of this evidence is that relationship conflict is reliably harmful, while task conflict's relation to performance is neither reliably harmful nor reliably helpful, and depends on conditions. De Dreu and Weingart (2003) also report that conflict was more negatively related to performance in complex tasks such as decision making and projects.

De Dreu and Weingart (2003) found that the link between task conflict and team performance was less negative when the two conflict types were only weakly correlated. This suggests that a team benefits from naming which type is occurring and from keeping a task disagreement from spilling into a relationship conflict.

## Principled Negotiation

{/* Source tracking: Fisher and Ury (1981), the four principles of principled negotiation; White (1984) and Menkel-Meadow (2006) via Wikipedia (2026), Getting to Yes. */}

Fisher and Ury (1981) presented principled negotiation, a method built on four principles.

1. **Separate the people from the problem.** Discuss the design, not the author. Fisher and Ury list listening only to rebut, rather than to understand, as a common communication problem.
2. **Focus on interests, not positions.** A position is what someone says they want. An interest is the reason behind it. Two engineers may both insist on one framework while one wants fast onboarding and the other wants long-term maintainability.
3. **Invent options for mutual gain.** Once interests are visible, a team can search for solutions that serve both.
4. **Insist on using objective criteria.** Options are judged against a standard both sides accept, such as a latency target or maintenance cost, rather than by who has more authority.

Principled negotiation is meant to keep a task disagreement from becoming personal, but the evidence for it is weak. White (1984) suggested that *Getting to Yes* "relies on anecdotal evidence", and Fisher replied that the book gives advice (Wikipedia, 2026). Menkel-Meadow (2006) wrote that the factors behind successful and failed negotiations are still unclear.

## Example: A Stalled Migration

This hypothetical case uses invented roles. In week 1, two senior engineers, Ana and Ben, disagree about whether to migrate a service to a new message broker now or after a planned refactoring. Their positions are fixed: "now" against "later". In week 3 the team lead asks each to state the other's concern. Ana fears a half-finished migration will leave two systems running for months. Ben fears that waiting for the refactoring will push the migration back a quarter, keeping the old broker running that much longer. Their interests overlap: both want the old broker retired soon.

With the interests visible, the lead proposes an option for mutual gain, a time-boxed prototype on one non-critical path, and the pair agree on an objective criterion: the prototype must sustain the current message rate without a serialization change. In week 5 it fails that test, which shows that part of the refactoring is a prerequisite. Ana and Ben adopt a plan that sequences the work, and the dispute ends without the lead overruling either engineer. Moving from positions to interests and then to a shared criterion turned a personal standoff into a test result.

## Exercise

Choose a recent disagreement on a team you know and classify it as task, process, or relationship conflict. Write each party's stated position and, for each, one sentence beginning "They want this because". Name one option that serves both interests and one objective criterion for judging it. A good answer separates positions from interests, and its criterion could be measured or checked by both sides.

# Frameworks for Leading a Team

A new lead has to decide how much to direct, how much to serve, how to motivate, and how much leadership to share. Four frameworks answer these questions, and they differ a great deal in the strength of their evidence.

## Situational Leadership

{/* Source tracking: Hersey & Blanchard (1969), pp. 26–34; Blanchard, Zigarmi & Zigarmi (1985); Thompson & Vecchio (2009), abstract. */}

Hersey and Blanchard (1969) presented the Life Cycle Theory of Leadership. It describes four quadrants of leader behaviour, defined by high or low task behaviour and high or low relationships behaviour, and matches them to the follower's "maturity". The article reports that empirical studies point to no one best style of leadership, and that as followers mature the appropriate leader behaviour needs less structure and later less socio-emotional support. The quadrants are numbered one to four and carry no style names in the 1969 text.

The labels Directing, Coaching, Supporting and Delegating belong to a later variant, SLII, introduced by Blanchard, Zigarmi and Zigarmi (1985). SLII replaces maturity with development levels described by competence (ability, knowledge and skill) and commitment (confidence and motivation).

The evidence is weak. Thompson and Vecchio (2009) tested three versions of the theory on survey data from 357 banking employees and 80 supervisors in ten Norwegian financial institutions. The revised 2007 version predicted subordinate performance and attitudes less well than the original 1972 statement, and an alternative version did not add substantially to the variance explained. This is one sample from one industry, so the result does not show that the idea of adapting to the follower is wrong. It shows that the specific matching prescriptions are not well supported.

## Servant Leadership

{/* Source tracking: Greenleaf (2002), p. 27; Spears (1998), pp. 3–6; van Dierendonck (2011), abstract. */}

Greenleaf popularized the phrase in his essay "The Servant as Leader" (1970). His own criterion is a question about the people being served: do they "grow as persons", and do they become "healthier, wiser, freer, more autonomous" and more likely themselves to become servants (Greenleaf, 2002, p. 27). The familiar list of ten characteristics, including listening, empathy, stewardship and building community, is a distillation by Spears (1998), not Greenleaf's own list.

van Dierendonck (2011) reviewed the field and proposed that servant leadership shows in leaders who join a drive to lead with a wish to serve, through empowering and developing people, humility, authenticity, interpersonal acceptance, stewardship and providing direction. The evidence is still emerging: the review expects a high-quality leader-follower relationship, trust and fairness to be the key mediating processes, but it proposes them rather than demonstrating them.

## Transformational and Transactional Leadership

{/* Source tracking: Burns (1978); Judge & Piccolo (2004), abstract. */}

Burns (1978) distinguished transactional leadership, an exchange between leader and follower, from transformational leadership. In Judge and Piccolo's (2004) meta-analysis, transactional leadership was measured as contingent reward and management by exception.

Judge and Piccolo (2004) combined 626 correlations from 87 sources. Transformational leadership had an overall validity of .44. Contingent reward (.39) and laissez-faire leadership (-.37) had the next highest overall relations, and management by exception was inconsistent. For several criteria contingent reward had stronger relations than transformational leadership. The two correlated .80, so the behaviours overlap in practice. The common claim that transactional leadership suits only simple, repetitive tasks is not supported by this abstract. Contingent reward was second strongest overall, and nothing in it concerns task simplicity. The meta-analysis is correlational, so it cannot show that leaders caused the outcomes.

## Shared Leadership

{/* Source tracking: Wang, Waldman & Zhang (2014), abstract. */}

Shared leadership, also called collective or distributed leadership, treats leadership as a process spread across team members. Wang, Waldman and Zhang (2014) cumulated 42 independent samples and found an overall relationship with team effectiveness of rho = .34. Shared traditional leadership (initiating structure and consideration) showed a lower relationship (.18) than shared new-genre leadership such as charismatic and transformational (.34). The effects were stronger when the work of team members was more complex, and shared leadership showed effects beyond those of vertical leadership. This suggests that, on complex work, a lead who spreads leadership across members can add to the team's effectiveness.

## Example: A New Tech Lead (Hypothetical)

Priya is promoted to tech lead in week 1. Dev, a new hire, has little experience with the codebase but is keen. Sam is a senior engineer who has begun to disengage after a reorganisation.

In SLII terms, Dev has low competence and high commitment, and Sam has high competence and wavering commitment. These readings are Priya's own hypotheses. She plans to give Dev specific guidance and frequent feedback, and to ask Sam what is behind the change before agreeing on goals. The caveat from Thompson and Vecchio (2009) applies: Priya treats these matches as hypotheses to check in conversation, not as rules.

By week 6 Priya applies Greenleaf's test as a question. Is Dev growing? Is Sam more autonomous? When Sam has taken the lead on the migration and Dev on the test tooling, the team has begun to share leadership. Because the migration is complex work, this fits the pattern Wang, Waldman and Zhang (2014) report.

## Exercise

Choose a team you have worked in. Write a short plan, about 150 words, for one named member. State which SLII development level (competence and commitment) you would assign and what evidence you have for it. Add one question based on Greenleaf's test that you could ask that person. Name one piece of leadership you would hand to someone else and say why the work is complex enough to justify it. A good answer separates your observation from your interpretation, treats the match as a hypothesis, and names what would change your mind.
